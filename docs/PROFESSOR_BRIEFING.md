## Executive summary

RL-Cleanse asks whether a learned policy can beat static rule-based filtering
for real-time sensor cleaning at the IoT edge, under the constraint that
actually binds there: the device receives telemetry **one reading at a time**
and cannot buffer the future.

Using the UCI Air Quality dataset (9,357 real hourly readings from a chemical
multisensor array deployed on an Italian road), we compare three methods on
identical held-out windows under identical constraints, across five fault
families, aggregated over five independently trained seeds.

**The answer is yes against the rule filter, with one important qualification.**

| Method | Mean absolute MAE | Mean relative MAE |
|---|---:|---:|
| Do nothing (pass through, carry forward on dropout) | 0.2170 | 1.000 |
| Rule filter, hyperparameters calibrated on the training split | 0.2170 | 1.000 |
| Rule filter, conventional Hampel cut-off $z{=}4$ | 0.4811 | 4.277 |
| **RL-Cleanse DQN** (5 seeds) | **0.2002** | 1.082 |

The learned policy reduces absolute error below every rule configuration and
below doing nothing. It removes **30.6%** of the error on high-amplitude spikes
with a cross-seed standard deviation of 0.0037 — a robust effect, not a lucky
run. **Neither rule configuration improves on doing nothing at all.**

The qualification: the policy over-corrects two fault families whose error was
already near zero, so an *equal-weight* aggregate across families comes out
slightly worse than inaction. Both aggregates are reported throughout, because
which one matters depends on the deployment.

## 1. Research question and contribution

### The question

> *Does a learned RL cleaning policy outperform static rule-based filtering when
> correcting noisy sensor readings under a strictly online, sequential
> constraint, where the agent sees one reading at a time and cannot access the
> full batch upfront?*

### What we claim

1. **The online constraint is an information barrier, not a mere
   inconvenience — and we measured it.** At time $t$, a single-sample spike and a
   genuine step change in the signal produce the *same* innovation. They separate
   only at $t+1$, when one reverts and the other persists. A causal filter does
   not have $t+1$. We show the consequence empirically: a grid search over the
   rule filter's rejection threshold on the training split is **monotone out to
   $z \to \infty$** — mean MAE 0.2739 at $z{=}12$, 0.2582 at $z{=}30$, 0.2561 at
   $z \to \infty$, which is exactly the pass-through number. On this channel, at
   these fault amplitudes, *no hard-thresholding filter beats doing nothing.*

2. **A learned policy sidesteps that barrier by not making the decision.** The
   action space includes a *soft* correction (`fix_type`: move the reading halfway
   to the rolling baseline). This trades a little error on genuine readings for a
   lot on spikes without ever having to classify which is which. A threshold
   filter has only accept-or-reject available. This is the mechanism behind the
   30.6% spike-family gain, and it is visible directly in the learned action
   distribution (notebook section 6).

3. **Magnitude-aware, outcome-based reward shaping.** The reward is proportional
   to the error the action actually removed, bounded on both sides, and — this
   matters — a function of *outcome only*, never of the generator's fault label.

### What we do not claim

- We do **not** claim the agent learns drift dynamics. Its drift improvement is
  5.2%, real but modest, and the rule filter's is zero by construction.
- We do **not** claim a uniform win over inaction. See section 4.3.
- We do **not** claim any hardware result. See section 5.

## 2. Dataset and methodological rigor

**Source.** UCI Air Quality (De Vito et al., 2008): a gas multisensor device
deployed at road level in an Italian city, March 2004 – April 2005. 9,357 hourly
instances. Target channel `CO(GT)`.

**The `-200` sentinel, handled honestly.** The published CSV encodes hardware
faults as `-200`. On `CO(GT)` this is **1,683 readings, 18.0% of the record**.
These are resolved by linear interpolation to give a continuous reference, but
**the interpolated positions are flagged and excluded from every metric**.
Scoring a denoiser at a timestep whose reference came from `numpy.interp`
measures agreement with an interpolator, not with a sensor. Windows are
additionally drawn to be at least 75% real measurement — a data-quality
criterion applied identically to every method, conditioned on no method's
output.

**Chronological splits, three of them.**

| Split | Hours | Purpose |
|---|---:|---|
| Train | 6,737 | Policy learning, and rule-filter calibration |
| Validation | 748 | Checkpoint selection **only** |
| Test | 1,872 | Held out; touched once, for the final table |

The train/test boundary is at hour 7,485 as specified. The validation split is
carved from the **end of the training region**, never from the test region.
No shuffling and no $k$-fold: both leak future trend into past training
instances, which invalidates any streaming claim.

**The baseline receives the same tuning budget as the agent.** Its
hyperparameters come from a grid search (`python -m backend.ml.calibrate_baseline`)
over 11 thresholds × 3 buffer lengths × 2 warm-up values on the **training split
only**. A hand-picked threshold reported without calibration is how a strawman
gets built.

## 3. Mathematical formulation (MDP)

$\mathcal{M} = \langle \mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, \mathcal{P}, \mathcal{R}, \gamma \rangle$

### 3.1 State space $\mathcal{S} \subset \mathbb{R}^{19}$

At step $t$, from the current packet plus five retained floats:

- reading-present flag; $\log(1+|y_t|)\,\mathrm{sign}(y_t)$; $\log(1+\sigma_t)$
- $(y_t - \mu_t)/(\sigma_t + \epsilon)$, $(y_t - \tilde y_t)/(\sigma_t + \epsilon)$,
  $(y_t - y_{t-1})/(\sigma_t + \epsilon)$, $(\mu_t - \tilde y_t)/(\sigma_t + \epsilon)$
- history fill level; time-of-day phase $\tau_t = (t \bmod 24)/24$; episode progress
- the $N{=}5$ buffer as $(y_{t-k} - \mu_t)/(\sigma_t + \epsilon)$
- four **heuristic** fault flags (missing, spike, drift, duplicate)

Rolling statistics are over the last $N=5$ values **the agent itself emitted**.
Deviations are divided by the rolling $\sigma$, so the policy is
**scale-relative**: it needs no dataset-wide normaliser a device could not
compute, and it transfers across channels with different dynamic ranges. A unit
test asserts this invariance.

**Two invariants are enforced in code, and asserted in the notebook.**
*No future access*: the observation at step $t$ is built from `dataset[t]` and
from already-emitted values; there is no code path for sample $t+1$.
*No oracle leakage*: the generator's `ground_truth`, `fault_type`,
`reference_imputed` and `is_corrupted` annotations are **stripped** from the
observation, so a downstream encoder cannot read them even by accident. The
agent sees exactly `{id, timestep, sensor, value}`.

### 3.2 Action space $\mathcal{A}$ (six discrete actions)

Semantics live in the environment, so each has one definition and is
unit-tested independently of any agent:

| Action | Rule |
|---|---|
| $a_0$ `skip` | $y_t^{\text{clean}} = y_t$ (a dropout stays missing, and the metric charges for it) |
| $a_1$ `remove_outlier` | rolling median $\tilde y_t$ |
| $a_2$ `fill_missing` | rolling median $\tilde y_t$ |
| $a_3$ `fix_type` | $0.5\,y_t + 0.5\,\mu_t$ — the **soft** correction |
| $a_4$ `remove_duplicate` | $y_{t-1} + (y_{t-1} - y_{t-2})$, trend extrapolation |
| $a_5$ `fix_category` | clamp $y_t$ into $[\min, \max]$ of the history |

With no history yet (step 0) every corrective action degrades gracefully to the
raw reading.

### 3.3 Reward $\mathcal{R}$

$$R_t = \begin{cases}
R_{\text{base}} + \min(0.45,\ 0.2\,\Delta e) & \Delta e > 0 \\
-0.15 - \min(0.35,\ 0.2\,|\Delta e|) & \Delta e \le 0 \\
+0.15 & \text{skip and } e_{\text{raw}} < 0.2
\end{cases}
\qquad \Delta e = e_{\text{raw}} - e_{\text{clean}}$$

$e_{\text{raw}} = |y_t - y^*_t|$ is the error of doing nothing; for a dropout it
is the error of the **carry-forward** value, so imputation is rewarded exactly
for beating carry-forward. Both magnitude terms are clipped, so one extreme
spike cannot dominate an episode's return and destabilise the Bellman targets.
The reward uses ground truth and is therefore a **training-time signal only**;
it is unavailable at deployment and never enters the observation.

### 3.4 Algorithm and hyperparameters

Double DQN, MLP $19 \to 128 \to 64 \to 6$ (11,206 parameters), Adam at
$3\times10^{-4}$, batch 64, $\gamma = 0.99$, Huber loss, gradient-norm clip 10,
target network refreshed every 5 epochs, replay capacity 10,000, 800 epochs
of 24-hour episodes cycling all five fault families.

Double DQN is a deliberate choice, made after observing the failure it fixes:
plain DQN's max over the target network lets the same noise that inflates an
action's value also select it, and behaviourally that showed up as the policy
over-correcting clean streams.

**One documented deviation from the master plan.** The plan specifies
$\epsilon$ decay $= 0.95$ per epoch. Applied literally, $\epsilon$ reaches its
0.02 floor at epoch 76, so an 800-epoch run would explore for its first 10%
only. We derive the decay rate from the run length so the schedule spans 70% of
training, preserving the plan's $1.0 \to 0.02$ endpoints. The literal value
remains available as an explicit override.

## 4. Empirical evaluation

30 held-out test windows of 24 hours per fault family, identical window seeds
for every method, 5 independently trained seeds for the RL column.

### 4.1 Results

| Fault family | Do-nothing MAE | Rule(cal) MAE | Rule(txt) MAE | RL MAE | RL std | Rule(cal) rel | Rule(txt) rel | **RL rel** | RL reduction |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Electrical spike | 0.2317 | 0.2317 | 0.4795 | **0.1608** | 0.0037 | 1.000 | 2.069 | **0.694** | **+30.6%** |
| Calibration drift | 0.2743 | 0.2743 | 0.5351 | **0.2600** | 0.0015 | 1.000 | 1.951 | **0.948** | +5.2% |
| Transmission dropout | **0.0627** | 0.0627 | 0.2861 | 0.0946 | 0.0018 | 1.000 | 4.559 | 1.507 | −50.7% |
| Duplicate packet | **0.0299** | 0.0299 | 0.3351 | 0.0401 | 0.0036 | 1.000 | 11.225 | 1.344 | −34.4% |
| Mixed real-world | 0.4863 | 0.4863 | 0.7697 | **0.4457** | 0.0126 | 1.000 | 1.583 | **0.916** | +8.4% |
| **Mean** | **0.2170** | 0.2170 | 0.4811 | **0.2002** | — | 1.000 | 4.277 | 1.082 | — |

`Rule(cal)` = grid-searched on the training split. `Rule(txt)` = conventional
Hampel cut-off $z{=}4$. `rel` = method MAE ÷ that family's own do-nothing MAE,
so 1.000 means no improvement and below 1.000 is a genuine gain.

### 4.2 What this supports

1. **RL-Cleanse outperforms rule-based filtering.** Mean absolute MAE 0.2002 vs
   0.2170 calibrated and 0.4811 textbook. The textbook filter *doubles to
   eleven-times* the error on individual families — a naively-tuned industrial
   filter is actively harmful on this signal.

2. **The rule filter cannot beat inaction, and this is measured, not asserted.**
   `Rule(cal) rel` is exactly 1.000 on every family: the calibrated gate
   essentially never fires, because the grid search found that not firing is
   optimal. This is the honest outcome of calibration, not a missing run.

3. **The spike-family gain is robust.** 30.6% error reduction with a cross-seed
   standard deviation of 0.0037 on an MAE of 0.1608 — roughly 2% relative spread
   across five independent training runs.

4. **Soft correction is the mechanism.** The learned policy leans on `fix_type`,
   the halfway-to-baseline action. It does not attempt to classify spikes, which
   is the thing the online constraint makes impossible.

### 4.3 What this does *not* support — three negative findings

**(a) The policy over-corrects benign streams.** On dropout (rel 1.507) and
duplicate (rel 1.344) it makes things worse. Both families have a do-nothing MAE
of 0.06 and 0.03: there is essentially nothing to win, and any action is a net
loss. The percentages look dramatic only because the denominator is near zero —
in absolute terms these are +0.032 and +0.010 on a signal whose window standard
deviation is around 1.1. The absolute columns are the honest ones here.

A mean hides its own variance, so here is the blunter question: on any given
24-hour window, is the policy more likely to improve the stream or damage it?
Over 750 episodes (30 windows × 5 seeds × 5 families), where Δ is the change in
window MAE and negative means the agent reduced error:

| Fault family | Improved | Unchanged | **Damaged** | Median Δ | Worst Δ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Spike | **63%** | 20% | 17% | −0.0590 | +0.0958 |
| Drift | 33% | 47% | 21% | 0.0000 | +0.1098 |
| Dropout | 10% | 20% | **70%** | +0.0145 | +0.2247 |
| Duplicate | 1% | 61% | **39%** | 0.0000 | +0.1935 |
| Mixed | **65%** | 9% | 27% | −0.0215 | +0.5292 |
| **All** | 34% | 31% | 35% | 0.0000 | +0.5292 |

Mean Δ across all 750 episodes is −0.0167, so the policy is net beneficial —
but only 34% of individual windows improve and 35% are damaged. The aggregate
gain is carried by a minority of windows containing large transients, where the
absolute saving is big, against a majority of small losses. On the two families
worth intervening in, spike and mixed, the policy improves roughly two windows
in three. On dropout it damages seven in ten.

This has a practical consequence for demonstration: **a single window chosen at
random is close to a coin flip and evidences nothing.** `scripts/demo_window.py`
therefore defaults to the spike window whose error reduction sits nearest the
family median (+14.4% against a median of +16.0%), prints these population
statistics alongside it, and exposes `--pick` to show the full candidate
distribution. Regenerate the table with
`python -m backend.ml.evaluate_benchmarks --num-windows 30`.

**(b) Validation did not predict test.** Validation relative-MAE averaged
**0.9725 ± 0.0104** across the five seeds (range 0.9575–0.9858) — a ~3%
improvement on inaction. Held-out test relative-MAE averaged about **1.01**
(per-seed: 1.0172, 0.9977, 0.9994, 1.0245, 1.0200). Selecting the best of ~32
validation evaluations per run is itself a selection bias, and this gap is its
size. The per-family test results are stable across seeds, but the aggregate
claim "better than inaction" **does not survive** the move to held-out data.

**(c) The specified spike amplitude is marginal for this channel.** At
2.5–4.0$\sigma$ of the window standard deviation, injected spikes land *inside*
the channel's own innovation envelope: genuine clean-signal innovations have a
99th-percentile robust $z$ of 8.8 and a maximum of 44 (measured over 200 clean
training windows). The faults are, by construction, hard to separate from the
signal. This is a property of the specified corruption model, and it caps what
any causal method can achieve. We kept the specified amplitudes rather than
raising them to flatter the results.

### 4.4 Sanity checks the pipeline runs on itself

- The rule filter's MAE on **perfectly uncorrupted** input is under 0.02. A
  filter that damages a clean signal cannot be trusted to report anything about
  a corrupted one, and two earlier revisions of this filter scored 0.240 and
  0.208 on this check.
- An **oracle-greedy** policy — one allowed to see the reward, and so the ground
  truth, when choosing — reaches 98.5% error reduction on spikes and 68% on
  drift. This confirms the action space is *capable*, so a weak learned result is
  a learning or detectability problem rather than an impossibility.
- A pass-through policy scores exactly 0.500 on the task grader by construction,
  and a perfect reconstruction scores 1.000. Both are asserted in tests.

### 4.5 Training cost

800 epochs in **83 seconds** on an RTX 4050 Laptop (6 GB), five seeds in about
seven minutes. The pipeline auto-detects CUDA and falls back to CPU unchanged;
the unit tests and the notebook both run CPU-only, which is how CI validates
them without a GPU.

**No physical hardware has been tested.** `docs/HARDWARE_SETUP.md` section 0 is
a table stating, per component, exactly what has been executed. In summary:

| Component | Status |
|---|---|
| Requisition list, live demo procedure | **Planned** |
| Circuit design and pinout | **Designed**, never wired |
| Wokwi / Tinkercad simulation | **Planned** — no simulation run is recorded, so none is claimed |
| ESP32 firmware sketch | **Written, not flashed** |
| Serial bridge `scripts/live_iot_inference.py` | **Implemented; replay-tested only** |
| On-device latency | **Not measured** |

The bridge runs end to end via `--replay`, which drives it from the UCI test
split; its `--port` serial path has never been connected to a board.

**Feasibility argument, and its limits.** The policy is an 11,206-parameter MLP
over a 19-dimensional input assembled from one packet plus five retained floats.
Inference is one forward pass, $O(1)$ memory, no lookahead, and no dataset-wide
normaliser. On the development host it measures **0.25 ms mean, 0.36 ms p95**.
That is a host-CPU number. An ESP32-class figure would require flashing the
board and timing it there. The arithmetic is small enough that on-device
inference is *plausible* — that is the claim, and it is not a measurement.

A further untested gap: the policy was trained on the UCI air-quality channel,
not on MQ-135 output, whose scale and noise characteristics differ. The
scale-relative encoder is the reason to expect some transfer. Transfer to that
sensor is unverified.

## 6. Anticipated questions

**Q: Your equal-weight aggregate is worse than doing nothing. Why is this not a
failed project?**
Because the research question is about rule-based filtering, and that comparison
is unambiguous: 0.2002 vs 0.2170 and 0.4811. The more interesting result is
*why* the rule filter cannot win — we traced it to a causal identifiability
limit and verified it with a threshold sweep out to infinity. The agent's
over-correction on near-clean families is a diagnosed, quantified weakness with
a clear fix (section 8), not an unexplained failure.

**Q: Why is the calibrated rule filter identical to doing nothing? Did you run
it?**
Yes, and the grid search output is committed to `reports/baseline_calibration.json`.
The calibration genuinely selects a gate so wide that it almost never fires,
because every narrower gate is worse. We report the textbook $z{=}4$
configuration alongside it precisely so the column is not degenerate.

**Q: Why not a Kalman filter?**
A Kalman filter needs explicit process and measurement noise covariances
($Q$, $R$). Burst spikes are non-Gaussian, dropouts are not a noise process at
all, and drift is a non-stationary mean shift. Fitting those parametrically on
this channel is its own research problem. That said, a Kalman baseline is a fair
request and is **not** in this submission — it belongs in section 8.

**Q: Why DQN rather than an LSTM or Transformer?**
Edge budget. A recurrent or attention model's memory and latency are hard to
justify on a microcontroller with sub-megabyte RAM. The 19-dimensional
hand-specified state already carries the temporal context a 5-step window
affords. We have not compared against a recurrent policy, so we claim only
suitability, not superiority.

**Q: How does it generalise to another sensor?**
Every deviation feature is divided by the rolling $\sigma$, so the policy is
scale-relative by construction and a unit test asserts the invariance. Actual
cross-channel transfer is untested.

**Q: Five seeds is not many.**
Agreed. The per-family results are tight across those five (spike std 0.0037),
but we report means rather than a paired significance test, which is the first
item in section 8.

## 7. A note on research integrity

An earlier revision of this briefing carried a results table reporting +19.9%
on spikes, 100% imputation on dropouts, and baseline MAE of 0.55–0.69. Those
numbers were not produced by a valid experiment. Auditing the pipeline found:

1. **The trained agent was doing nothing.** It selected `fix_category` on 110 of
   120 evaluation steps — an action the environment did not implement, so it
   never wrote a value. "RL MAE" equalled raw MAE because the RL output *was*
   the raw stream.
2. **Root cause: exploration was destroyed at epoch 10 of 40.**
   `set_training_mode(False)` zeroed $\epsilon$ and nothing restored it, so the
   first mid-training evaluation ended exploration permanently. The saved
   $\epsilon$ history shows the collapse.
3. **"100% imputation" was a metric artifact.** Un-imputed dropout timesteps were
   filtered out of the MAE average, so a policy that emitted *nothing* scored a
   perfect 0.0000.
4. **The baseline was a strawman, unintentionally.** Its 5-sample mean/std test
   at 2.8$\sigma$ fired on ordinary diurnal ramps, and it fed its own
   replacements back into its history buffer, collapsing the window to a
   constant. On *perfectly clean* input it scored 0.208 MAE — worse than the
   corruption it was meant to remove.
5. **Checkpoints were selected on the test split**, and training sampled only
   the first 680 of 7,485 training hours because the window index was a function
   of the epoch number.

A sixth defect was introduced *during* the repair and found afterwards, which is
worth recording because it is the same failure mode in a new place. The
best-checkpoint save in `train_dqn.py` sat outside the `if save:` guard, so every
run of the test suite — `train(epochs=12, save=False)` with the default empty
checkpoint suffix — silently overwrote `models/dqn_iot_stream_best.pt` with a
12-epoch CPU model. The published headline checkpoint was therefore a test
artifact, and it scored 0.2763 MAE on the spike family against a do-nothing
0.2317: **worse than inaction.** The test suite was corrupting the artifact the
project reports.

It was caught by a consistency check, not by inspection: the demo script's
per-window statistics for the spike family (8 of 30 windows improved)
contradicted the 5-seed analysis (63% improved), and the two figures could not
both be right. The results table was unaffected, because it aggregates the five
per-seed checkpoints rather than the headline copy, but
`plots/denoising_before_after.png` and the notebook's signal plot had been
generated with the bad model and were regenerated. The best weights are now held
in memory and written only when `save=True`, and two regression tests assert
that `train(save=False)` writes nothing at all.

All six are fixed, each has a named regression test, and the tests assert the
specific failure rather than the general area. The current table is worse than
the fabricated one on several rows. It is also real.

## 8. Limitations and future work

**Limitations.** Single dataset, single channel, single algorithm, five seeds.
The equal-weight aggregate does not beat inaction. Validation-to-test
generalisation is weak. 18% of the reference signal is interpolated (excluded
from metrics, but the remainder is not pristine). The reward's skip tolerance is
an absolute 0.2 in signal units, so the agent has no incentive to improve errors
already below it — which is why even an oracle-greedy policy reaches only +9.1%
on dropout. No hardware validation.

**Future work, in priority order.**

1. Paired significance testing across seeds and windows, replacing comparison of
   means.
2. An explicit cost asymmetry in the reward, or a learned abstention, to stop
   over-correction on near-clean streams. This is the clearest single path to
   making the aggregate claim hold.
3. A Kalman-filter and an offline-oracle baseline, to bracket the learned policy
   from both sides.
4. A spike-amplitude sweep to map the detectability boundary.
5. ESP32 latency and RAM measurement, replacing the feasibility argument with a
   number.
